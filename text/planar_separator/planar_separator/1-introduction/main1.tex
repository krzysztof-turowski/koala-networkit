\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}

The planar separator theorem is a fundamental result in graph theory that
states that any planar graph can be separated into two roughly equal parts
by removing a small number of vertices. This theorem has numerous
applications in various fields, including computer science, engineering,
and mathematics, serving as the baseline for designing highly efficient 
divide and conquer algorithms. The main idea of this thesis is to bridge the 
gap between the theoretical guarantees of the planar separator theorem and
practical performance of the algorithms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item At first we shall see the background for separator theorems in general
        and later explore planar graphs to see why they are special and how 
        we can use their properties to design efficient algorithms.
    \item In the second section we shall see the outline of the proof for 
        Lipton and Tarjan planar separator theorem with addition to a few 
        lemmas that will be the cornerstone for developing approximation 
        algorithms in the third section.
    \item In the third section we will see the practical consequences of 
        all the lemmas developed in the previous section. We shall explore 
        two algorithms for two different problems: maximum matching problems
        and minimum vertex cover problem which is $NP$-complete.
    \item In the last section we will focus on practical implementations of 
        the stated algorithms, and see how their performance scales with different
        topological families of planar graphs and different sizes.
\end{itemize}
\section{Background}
\begin{defn}
    A set $S \subseteq V(G)$ is a separator if induced graph $G^* = G - S$
    has at least two connected components $A,B \subseteq G^*$.
\end{defn}

One of the first times this concept was brought to a broader audience was
in \cite{jordan1869assemblages}, where Jordan proved that every free tree T
has a separator of size 1 (a single vertex), whose removal partitions T into
two or more connected components each of size at most $n/2$.

Through the years, more and more classes of graphs started to show some
interesting properties regarding vertex separators, so a general theorem was
assembled, which is called a $f(n)$-separator theorem:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:fnseparator}
    Let $S$ be a class of graphs closed under a subgraph relation (i.e. if
    $G_1 \in S$ and $G_2$ is a subgraph of $G_1$, then $G_2 \in S$). There
    exist constants $\alpha < 1$, $\beta > 0$ such that if $G$ is any n-vertex
    graph in $S$, the vertices of $G$ can be partitioned into three sets $A,B,C$
    such that no edge connects any vertex in $A$ and $B$, neither $A$ nor $B$
    have more than $\alpha n$ vertices and $C$ contains no more than $\beta
    f(n)$ vertices
\end{theorem}

Such theorem regarding planar graphs, where $f(n) = O(\sqrt{n}),$ $ \alpha = 2/3,
$ $\beta = 1$ is called planar separator theorem. First breakthrough in that area
was in 1951 \cite{ungar1951theorem}, when Ungar proved a slightly weaker theorem
with $f(n) = O(\sqrt{n} \log^{3/2}n)$. A small improvement to that was later
achieved in 1973 by George \cite{george1973nested} where he proved that any
grid graph of size $n$ has a $O({\sqrt{n}})$ separator. However, the first to
prove planar separator theorem in the full form were Lipton and Tarjan in 1977
\cite{liptonSeparatorTheoremPlanar1979}, where it was proven that for each
planar graph a separator of size at most $2\sqrt{2} \sqrt{n}$ exists.
In many articles later after Lipton and Tarjan, the constant $\beta$ was
reduced, however its lower bound is $(\sqrt{4\pi \sqrt{3}})/3$ which was proven
by Djidjev in \cite{djidjev1982problem}.
Djidjev established this absolute limit not by looking at flat grids,
but by constructing worst-case planar graphs based on the triangulation
of a sphere, proving mathematically that no algorithm can separate
these dense spherical meshes with fewer vertices.
